GNOSTIC LIFE · ETHICS AND COMPASSION

Not feeding on suffering

Vegetarian and vegan eating on the Gnostic path

You do not have to believe in Gnosis to recognize an animal’s suffering. You simply have to refuse to look away. But anyone who claims to seek spiritual liberation should have even more reason to question the suffering they help to cause.

Suffering needs no justification

You do not need to belong to a religious tradition to understand that an animal can suffer. Fear, pain, and the desire to live do not become irrelevant simply because they belong to a creature different from ourselves. When we see a living being trying to escape death, we are faced not with a theory but with a concrete reality.

We usually consider it unjust when someone inflicts avoidable suffering on us. Why, then, should we regard it as normal to impose such suffering on creatures with even fewer means of defending themselves? It is a simple but uncomfortable question. For that very reason, it deserves to be faced without immediately searching for an excuse to avoid it.

The contradiction the Gnostic path places before us

In the dualist vision inspiring this path, the material world is marked by predation, corruption, and death. Here, life is often sustained through the destruction of other life. We do not attribute this law to the good Father: we recognize it as part of the condition from which Spirit longs to be freed.

But if we denounce the violence of this world while continuing to support it, when we could avoid doing so, out of habit or pleasure, what really changes in our lives? We cannot claim to turn away from the Demiurge’s logic while accepting it unquestioningly in the everyday act of eating.

Gnosis should not stop at words. Understanding that never touches our behaviour risks becoming merely another idea with which we identify.

Not feeding on the suffering of others

For me, giving up meat is not a sacrifice to display but a choice of consistency. Behind food of animal origin there may have been a creature that wanted to live, experienced fear, and had no opportunity to decide its own fate.

The fact that a practice is widespread, ancient, or socially accepted does not automatically make it right. Many habits are passed down without ever being truly examined. Breaking conditioning also means finding the courage to look at what we have learned not to see.

I do not claim that one dietary choice can erase all the world’s suffering. We can, however, avoid contributing to the suffering that directly depends on us, as far as we are practically able.

Vegetarianism and veganism: a principle, not a competition

Vegetarianism rejects eating animals’ flesh. Veganism extends that concern to the exploitation that can accompany the production of milk, eggs, and other animal-derived products.

The distinction matters because suffering does not end with killing. Farming conditions, the separation of young animals from their mothers, and the use of animals as instruments of production also deserve scrutiny.

Yet I do not want to turn compassion into a ranking of spiritual superiority. Personal circumstances differ, and no one can fully know those of others. For me, the direction remains clear: seek to reduce avoidable suffering as much as possible, without using difficulties as an excuse to abandon every attempt at change.

A thread running through history

Abstaining from meat is not foreign to the history of dualist traditions. Manichaeans placed great importance on dietary discipline, especially among their Elect. Cathars, too, particularly their spiritual ministers known as good men and good women, observed restrictions that included abstaining from meat, although within a different doctrinal and practical framework.

This does not mean that all Gnostics in the past followed the same rules, nor that we can simply equate their disciplines with contemporary veganism. It means, more modestly, that the relationship between food, violence, and spiritual life is an old question that still deserves to be taken seriously.

Compassion cannot be selective

It is easy to be moved by an animal living beside us and forget those we never encounter. Yet pain does not change its nature according to how familiar we are with the one experiencing it.

If spiritual inquiry teaches us anything, it should make us less indifferent, not more skilled at justifying suffering. The point is not to hate people who eat meat or to condemn individuals. It is to stop hiding the moral question behind habit, tradition, or convenience.

Firmness about a principle can coexist with respect for those who do not share it. Compassion would lose its meaning if it became merely another opportunity to look down on others.

A matter of conscience

I did not stop eating meat because someone told me to. I simply reached a point where I could no longer accept that an animal should die for my pleasure. From then on, carrying on as before would have meant turning away from something I had come to understand.

I do not claim that this choice makes anyone spiritually superior. But neither do I want to hide behind the word “choice” and pretend that every choice carries the same weight. If we can avoid having a living being killed to feed us, its suffering is no longer merely an unavoidable fact of life. It is also our responsibility.

Our aim, during this brief passage through the world, should be to cause as little suffering as possible. We know very well that in this infernal world we cannot even take a step or draw a breath without affecting other forms of life in some way. There is no absolute innocence in matter. But precisely because we cannot avoid all harm, we have all the more reason to refuse to cause suffering deliberately when we can avoid it.

Knowledge must become life

No one attains spiritual liberation merely by not eating meat. But a search that speaks of liberation while deliberately ignoring the suffering of the most vulnerable beings leaves a profound contradiction unresolved.

In our view, the good Father is not the principle of oppression. Drawing nearer to him also means learning to recognize what in our conduct still belongs to the laws of this world.

We cannot change the nature of matter on our own. We can, however, choose not to celebrate its cruelty or feed it unnecessarily. For me, this is not a secondary matter in Gnosis: it is one of its most concrete consequences.

This page offers an ethical and spiritual reflection, not medical advice or a claim that all Gnostic traditions followed the same dietary rules.
