GNOSTIC CATECHESIS · CHAPTER I

Breaking Conditioning: The First Step Towards Gnosis

Before seeking, learn to question

None of us is born with a fully formed view of the world. We grow up immersed in our family’s beliefs, the culture of our time, and the habits and fears passed down to us. Long before we can choose, we have already learned what we should desire, fear, admire, and consider normal. These layers of conditioning become so familiar that they seem part of our nature. Yet the first step towards Gnosis is precisely to question them.

Breaking conditioning does not mean automatically rejecting everything we have received. It means no longer accepting something as true simply because we were taught it. A belief may be ancient, shared by millions, and defended by powerful institutions, yet remain a belief. Inner knowledge begins when we find the courage to look without constantly asking permission to do so.

See the world without justifying it

We are often told that the world is fundamentally good, that suffering always serves a purpose, and that every tragedy conceals a higher plan. Yet observing nature makes it difficult to sustain this idea without looking away. Material life feeds on other life. Every creature struggles to survive, often at another’s expense. Illness, decay, fear, and death are not exceptions: they are part of how this world ordinarily works.

This does not prevent us from recognizing the beauty of a landscape, the tenderness of a gesture, or the joy of an encounter. But we must not use those moments to erase everything else. A spirituality that must deny suffering in order to defend the goodness of creation ultimately asks people not to see what is before their eyes.

The good Father and the creator of the world

In the absolute dualist vision guiding this path, the good Father is not the author of the material universe. He belongs to the reality of Spirit, to the Light that knows no corruption, violence, or death. Material creation, with its laws and its need to consume and destroy, belongs instead to another principle, which we call the Demiurge.

This distinction profoundly changes our understanding of spiritual life. We are not called to thank the Father for a world built on suffering, nor to regard every painful event as a test from him. The Father has no need to torture creatures to teach them to love him. If we recognize him as Goodness, we must have the courage not to attribute to him what is contrary to Goodness.

Here dualism does not arise from a desire to oppose two names, but from the need to distinguish two irreconcilable natures: Spirit and matter.

Captivity and the memory of our origin

If our origin is spiritual, why do we find ourselves in a material condition? This is one of the central questions of Gnosis. Human beings experience the body, needs, desires, time, and death; yet they may also feel a longing that no possession can satisfy.

In the language of this path, the Soul is involved in earthly existence, while the Spirit points towards the reality of the Father. These are not simply two words for the same thing. Understanding the distinction helps us avoid confusing every impulse of the psyche, every emotion and desire, with the voice of our spiritual origin.

The world constantly draws us outward. It teaches us to identify with our possessions, our roles, and the image others have of us. When these identifications falter, we feel we are losing ourselves. Perhaps we are only discovering how little we know of what we truly are.

Do not replace one conditioning with another

There is a danger even on the Gnostic path: leaving behind an inherited doctrine only to cling to a new one with the same rigidity. Repeating words such as Demiurge, Spirit, or liberation does not yet amount to knowing. Even an explanation that seems convincing can become a cage if we stop questioning it and ourselves.

This is why study must be accompanied by self-observation. When we react angrily to criticism, seek others’ approval, or defend an opinion simply because it is ours, we can see the very conditioning we claim to be overcoming. Gnosis is not a collection of answers to display, but a work of clarification.

Silence and the first exercise

We can begin simply. Each day, for a few minutes, set aside distractions and observe what happens in the mind. There is no need to force silence, suppress thoughts, or chase extraordinary experiences. It is enough to notice how quickly a memory, fear, or desire can seize our attention.

Then ask: have I truly understood the belief I am defending, or have I merely inherited it? And again: does what I now regard as indispensable really belong to my deepest nature, or is it a need constructed by the world I live in?

Answers need not arrive immediately. Sometimes the most important work is to remain with the question long enough, without immediately filling the silence with a reassuring formula.

The beginning of a return

Breaking conditioning does not automatically make us free. It is a beginning, not a completion. But without this first movement, it is hard to distinguish the search for truth from the desire to feel safe inside an explanation.

The path we propose does not ask us to hate living beings or despise those who suffer in matter. On the contrary, recognizing our shared condition should make us more attentive, more restrained, and less inclined to cause further suffering. We are not here to celebrate the prison, but neither are we here to worsen the captivity of others.

If there is a call within us towards the Homeland of Light, perhaps the first sign of awakening is this: to stop confusing the world’s voice with our deepest voice. An authentic search can begin here, not to improve the appearance of our chains, but to understand them and one day move beyond them.
