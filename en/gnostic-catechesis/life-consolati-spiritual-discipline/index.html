GNOSTIC CATECHESIS · CHAPTER 9

The Life of the Consolati and Spiritual Discipline

Discipline is not a method for feeling at one with creation. In this infernal world, merely living entails some involuntary involvement in the suffering of other beings. We cannot claim an impossible material purity; we can, however, refuse to inflict avoidable suffering deliberately. During our brief passage here, our task is to leave as little suffering behind as possible, without forgetting that this world is not our homeland.

Living in accordance with what we have understood

Cathar tradition placed great importance on the conduct of its spiritual ministers. It was not enough to affirm the existence of the good Father: one had to try to live according to a principle different from domination. Our catechesis takes up this orientation without turning it into a list of merits to display.

Nonviolence

If we recognize that matter is governed by laws of predation, we must avoid imitating them ourselves. Nonviolence does not mean indifference towards victims or refusing to protect them. It means rejecting cruelty, revenge, and the pleasure of inflicting pain. This is a demanding discipline, beginning with the most ordinary words, gestures, and reactions.

Animals and diet

Compassion cannot stop at the boundaries of our species. In our perspective, vegetarianism is a fundamental ethical consequence of the Gnostic path, and veganism deepens its reach by questioning the exploitation of animals as well. This is not a competition in purity. It means avoiding, as far as possible, suffering that depends on our choices.

Simplicity

The world urges us to seek something new at every turn: an object, recognition, pleasure. Simplicity is neither voluntary destitution nor contempt for necessities. It is the freedom not to let possessions define our identity. We can use the tools of the world without making them the centre of our lives.

Sincerity

Lying for convenience, manipulating people, or using spirituality to gain power are incompatible with the path we propose. Sincerity does not require brutality: we can tell the truth respectfully. And we must learn to recognize the lies we tell ourselves before judging those of others.

Prayer

Prayer addressed to the good Father is not a contract for material success. We do not ask him to govern the world’s laws in our favour as though he were their author. Prayer can recall our origin, express gratitude for the Light, and keep the direction of our path alive.

Meditation

Daily silence helps us recognize the mind’s automatic patterns. There is no need to seek extraordinary experiences. We observe anger, fear, desire, and the need for approval without denying them. This exercise does not magically erase conditioning, but it can make these forces less dominant over our actions.

Work and society

Living in the world entails concrete responsibilities: eating, working, and helping those who depend on us. Detachment does not mean abandoning people or neglecting health. It means not identifying life’s value with money, prestige, or competition. A spirituality that makes us irresponsible towards others has lost its direction.

No spiritual superiority

Discipline can become vanity. Someone who is vegetarian, meditates, or studies ancient texts may begin to feel superior to others. This is another form of attachment. Firmly rejecting violence does not require contempt for people; rather, it should make us more capable of understanding each person’s difficulties.

The path in ordinary life

We need not wait for a solemn occasion to put these principles into practice. We can begin with how we treat an animal, respond to an insult, use money, or listen to someone who suffers. Gnosis becomes concrete when our small decisions no longer constantly contradict what we say we seek.

Towards the return

Discipline is not an end in itself. It helps us avoid strengthening captivity. In the final chapter we will speak of the Homeland of Light and the meaning of liberation, without confusing them with a material reward or a promise of power.

Sources and further reading

This is a contemporary reading of radical dualism; historical Gnostic and Cathar traditions did not all share the same teachings.
