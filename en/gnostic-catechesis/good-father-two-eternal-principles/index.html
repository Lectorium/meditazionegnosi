GNOSTIC CATECHESIS · CHAPTER II

The Good Father and the Two Eternal Principles

We do not ask the good Father to justify the suffering of the world. Instead, we begin by recognizing that this suffering does not come from him.

A question we cannot avoid

If the Father is infinitely good, where does evil come from? I do not mean only human cruelty, wars, or injustice. I mean the illness that strikes a child, an animal devoured alive, suffering that precedes any moral choice. Pain does not enter the world only when human beings do wrong: it is already woven into the workings of nature.

For centuries, we have been asked to reconcile this with an all-powerful God who created everything. We have heard about trials, mysteries, and a higher good we cannot see. But an answer that turns every atrocity into something good eventually empties the word “good” of meaning. Our catechesis begins from another conviction: the good Father did not create this material order.

Good cannot be the source of Evil

A truly good principle does not deliberately produce what is contrary to its own nature. A clear spring cannot also be the source of poisoned water. This image, close to the reasoning of the Book of the Two Principles, invites us to judge a cause by its fruits as well.

We therefore do not hold the Father responsible for death, decay, or the laws forcing living beings to consume one another. Nor do we attribute indirect responsibility to him: we do not believe he designed suffering as a necessary school or allows it to obtain something from his creatures.

If the Father is love, his Kingdom cannot be founded on fear. If he is life, he cannot need death. If he is Light, he cannot be the source of darkness.

Two eternal principles, not a rebellious angel

Here lies the heart of our radical, or absolute, dualism. There are two original and irreconcilable principles: Good and Evil. The principle of Evil does not come from the Father; it is not his fallen son or a good angel who one day chose rebellion. It cannot be a product of Good, because that would make Good responsible, at least ultimately, for the existence of Evil.

To say that both principles are eternal means neither received existence from the other. It does not mean they are equal in dignity or value. The Father is the fullness of Good and spiritual life; the opposing principle is the root of corruption, division, and coercion. Their opposition is not a struggle between two powers deserving equal worship. We recognize and seek only the good Father.

This position distinguishes our catechesis from mitigated dualism, according to which evil originated in a creature that was initially good. For us, that explanation does not solve the problem: it merely pushes it back.

The unknown Father and the Homeland of Light

The Father is not an earthly ruler magnified infinitely. He is not a jealous lawgiver, a judge demanding sacrifices, or a power to appease. Our tradition recognizes him as the origin of all that is eternal, luminous, and spiritual: the Homeland of Light, where there is no illness, oppression, or death.

We call him “unknown” not because he is absent, but because he does not coincide with the image of God the world has taught us to imagine. Our categories, formed within matter and time, cannot contain him. We also distrust excessively human depictions of divinity: they risk turning the Father into merely a more powerful master.

For us, his call appears not in threats but in the desire for truth, in compassion, and in the longing for a reality free of violence that can arise within the human being.

The creator of this world is not the Father

In our catechesis, we call the Demiurge the principle that fashions the material order. He rules a world subject to birth and destruction, where every form wears away and no body escapes time. This does not mean every living being is evil: a suffering creature is not guilty of the law under which it lives. We distinguish the system of coercion from its victims.

We must also avoid confusing beauty with the goodness of the entire cosmic order. A forest can be magnificent while concealing hunger, parasitism, and death. Beauty does not erase suffering; sometimes it merely makes suffering less visible to us.

The next chapter examines the Demiurge and the laws of material creation. For now, the essential principle is that the good God must not be confused with the creator of this universe.

The distinction between the two fathers

In our reading of the Gospels, Christ’s words create a break with the idea that the power ruling this world is identical with the heavenly Father. In John’s Gospel, Jesus speaks of a Kingdom that is “not of this world” (18:36); elsewhere we find the “ruler of this world” (12:31; 14:30). We read these expressions in light of the distinction between spiritual origin and material dominion.

We do not claim this is the only possible reading of Christian texts: it is the dualist reading guiding our path. The medieval Cathar Book of the Two Principles develops the argument that Good and Evil cannot arise from a single root. The text is available here.

For this reason, we do not automatically identify the lawgiving and punitive God described in many passages of the Old Testament with the Father announced by Christ. This is not a judgment of people who follow other traditions, but a theological distinction that is decisive for us. Our criticism concerns an image of divinity and the power attributed to it, not a people.

The problem of free will

The problem of evil is often answered by saying that God allowed human beings to choose freely. But even if this explains some human actions, it does not explain suffering before humanity existed, animal disease, hunger, or biological death. It does not explain why a universe should have been built so that innocent beings inevitably suffer.

Radical dualism therefore refuses to blame creatures for the very existence of Evil. We can be responsible for our actions, for what we inflict or try to prevent; but we did not invent the cruel laws that preceded us. Moral responsibility remains important, but it cannot justify the structure of the world.

Why Evil cannot be merely the absence of Good

Another explanation describes evil as the mere privation of good, an emptiness without its own reality. Yet in our experience evil does not appear only as a lack. Mechanisms actively produce pain, organisms are compelled to prey on others, and bodies are subject to degeneration. This is not an occasional defect in an otherwise perfect creation.

The dualist perspective interprets this structure as the expression of a principle distinct from the Father. This is a spiritual and metaphysical position, not a scientific demonstration: science can describe natural processes, while we ask here about their meaning and ultimate origin.

Our origin is not our prison

If everything were merely matter, our destiny would coincide entirely with the body’s fate. But Gnosis announces another possibility: what belongs to the Father within us does not originate in the laws of this world and is not destined to be possessed by them forever.

The earlier Chiesa Catara catechesis spoke of the Homeland of Light, the separation of Soul and Spirit, and the return to the Father’s Home. We will revisit these ideas in the chapters on the fall and the human condition. For now, the important point is that liberation does not mean making matter immortal, but recognizing what within us does not belong to it.

What changes in everyday life

The distinction between the two principles is not meant to cultivate hatred of the world or contempt for those living in it. On the contrary, if we recognize suffering as a condition imposed upon living beings, we should feel closer to those who endure it. We cannot denounce the violence of the material system and then reproduce it indifferently through our choices.

From this arise nonviolence, the refusal to cause avoidable suffering to animals, simplicity, sincerity, and care for the most vulnerable. These are not moral decorations added to doctrine. They are an attempt not to serve, through our actions, the very thing from which we say we wish to be freed.

We do not claim to be pure or entirely free of bodily necessities. But we can try not to add needlessly to the pain already present in the world.

An exercise in discernment

Take a few minutes in silence and consider two images: on one side, an act of compassion performed without expecting a reward; on the other, violence inflicted on a defenceless being. Ask whether it is truly coherent to attribute both realities, in the same sense, to a single perfectly good principle.

Do not look for a formula to repeat. Observe which justifications immediately arise, which ideas you have accepted without examination, and which questions remain open. Catechesis offers a direction; inner work begins when that question becomes your own.

Towards the next chapter

The good Father is not the creator of the material prison. Evil is not an accident born from Good, but a distinct, original principle. And Spirit does not find its homeland in the laws of this world.

Once this distinction is understood, the next question arises: how does the Demiurge act, and why is material creation subject to division, desire, and death? That will be the subject of Chapter III.
