MEDITAZIONE E GNOSI

The Legacy of Gnosis

From antiquity to the Middle Ages: a recurring question

A history of many voices

From ancient Persia to the Hellenistic Mediterranean, from teachers of the early Christian centuries to medieval communities, this historical journey crosses very different worlds. Zoroaster, Plato, Hermes, Simon Magus, Basilides, Valentinus, Marcion, Mani, the Bogomils, and the Cathars did not belong to one school. Bringing them together does not erase their differences; it traces recurring questions about the human condition.

The problem of the world

Many of these traditions ask why existence is marked by suffering and why the visible world seems so far from perfect goodness. Their answers differ: an ignorant creator, two primordial principles, or a material order from which one should distance oneself. What they share is not a single formula but a refusal to regard immediate reality as the final word on human destiny. Our own position is more precise: in absolute dualism, Good and Evil are primordial and irreconcilable principles; the good Father did not create this world of predation and death. This is our conviction, not a doctrine to be attributed indiscriminately to every thinker discussed here.

Knowledge and awakening

Gnosis gives knowledge a meaning beyond ordinary instruction. Knowing can mean remembering one’s origin, recognizing the ignorance governing everyday life, and transforming how one exists. This inner dimension appears in myths of Sophia, the figure of Ennoia, Mani’s imprisoned Light, and the practices of later communities. These symbols are not interchangeable, but each invites us to look beyond appearances.

Conscience and authority

The history of Gnostic and dualist movements is also a history of conflicts with religious and political authorities. Not all dissent was identical, nor did every persecution have the same causes. Yet a tension recurs: can spiritual truth be imposed from outside? What place remains for individual conscience when an institution claims the right to determine what everyone must believe?

An open-ended journey

These pages do not claim to exhaust such a vast history. They offer a framework for exploring teachers, texts, and communities, while the video series follows individual episodes. A fuller study of doctrinal differences and their implications belongs to another kind of work. What remains here is an invitation: do not confuse knowledge with repeating formulas, but keep asking what we are and what we seek.
